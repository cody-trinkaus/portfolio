Section 01The Research Setup

In summer 2025 I joined research on whether AI can help with learning, reflection and metacognition in design education. My role was the reflective guinea pig. I picked five design competencies to grow, then explored them by building a computer vision bird identification tool, with AI as both creative partner and primary coder. I wrote three reflections on each competency over the summer and a final reflection on the whole project.

The bird identifier was the vehicle and the competencies were the point. Several of my definitions changed as I worked, and those changes are the clearest record of what I learned:

Section 02Process

I started with a process log. It recorded failures and their fixes, systems-thinking epiphanies, general notes, and "process interrupts": reflections triggered by transitions between tasks or by random timers. The log opened with the questions that framed the work:

"We have entered a new era, akin to the transition of the industrial revolution … Should we even pursue this technology? Are there only certain applications that can bring positive change? … This is an opportunity to reinvent the wheel."Opening of my process log

The plan ran in three stages: get computer vision and identification working, then build an interface, then build a relationship between the system and the person using it.

AI as primary coder

AI wrote much of the code. I made sure I understood how it was organized and why it worked, and I did periodic deep dives into specific parts of the system: model training, computer vision and the interface. Writing that down mattered for the research too, because it pushed me to reflect on what originality means when you let AI write your code.

Iteration 01Seeing Birds

The first version was a motion-activated computer vision system built from traditional techniques: pre-processing, feature extraction, dimensionality reduction and a random forest classifier. I chose that over deep learning because a Raspberry Pi has very little computing power.

Even the lighter approach ran into hard limits:

  • Data: I couldn't scrape a dataset big enough to train it well. An API like iNaturalist would have been ideal, but it and other large bird databases weren't publicly available to me.
  • Camera: a 10 fps webcam made birds that were far away or in bad lighting impossible to recognize.

To keep the concept moving, I added an adjustable confidence threshold so the system could recognize birds from photos and other objects. That let me build and test everything downstream by holding pictures of birds up to the camera.

Iteration 02Bird Stories

With identification working behind a basic interface, I moved to the relationship stage. Stories shape memory, learning and personal connection, so each time a bird was identified, the system used the Anthropic API to write a 200-word story about it. Non-migratory birds got stories about home and local ecosystems. Migratory birds got stories about travel and discovery.

This taught me how to wire AI-generated content into a live system. It also showed me the stories weren't worth reading: they were repetitive and unpleasant. If AI was going to tell people about the birds they saw, it should be in short facts, not long prose.

Iteration 03Flockr

Brainstorming with Claude as a creative partner, I landed on Flockr, a mock bird dating site. For every bird identified, Flockr generates a Tinder-style profile with an age, a match percentage, a bio, stats, territory, interests and red flags, plus Pass and Love buttons. The goal was to teach people about birds in a way that was engaging and funny.

Flockr leaned hard into building a relationship (pun intended) between people and birds. But it was a fun gimmick more than an impactful technology.

Iteration 04Bird Discovery

Looking for a more meaningful application, I thought about the complex systems that make up the natural world, and how they reach into our yards and shared spaces. Biodiversity is a key indicator of habitat health, so I shifted the app's focus from individual birds to species. The result was Pacific Northwest Bird Discovery.

The app keeps a collection of the species you've discovered. From the number and variety of birds identified, it calculates a habitat health score and a biodiversity score, suggests habitat improvements and awards achievements. I kept the per-bird cards, rewritten as short, funny species facts: the lesson from Iteration 02.

The scores aren't a perfect measure of habitat health. With more research, and a vision system that could also recognize plants and water, they could become genuinely useful to anyone trying to improve habitat for birds.

Section 03Reflection

Each iteration answered a question the one before it raised. The dashboard proved the Pi could see birds, if barely. The stories showed that AI content has to earn its place, and that short facts beat long prose. Flockr proved a relationship with birds could be fun before it was useful. Bird Discovery tried to make it useful by tying each sighting to the health of a system bigger than one bird.

The biggest constraints were physical and practical: a slow camera, a small computer and no access to large bird datasets. Working around them, instead of waiting for better hardware, is what moved the concept forward. Leaning on AI to write code let me get further in one summer than I could have alone. Keeping a process log and doing deep dives kept that from turning into code I couldn't explain. And watching my competency definitions shift was the most concrete evidence I've had of my own learning: physical computing grew from interacting with a user to interacting with the world, and systems thinking grew to include the mental models inside a system.

If I took it further, I'd start with the inputs: a better camera, a real dataset and a vision model that sees the whole habitat, not just the birds in it.
