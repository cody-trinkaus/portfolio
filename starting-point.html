Section 01Value Statement

What value does Starting Point offer? Starting Point delivers value beyond sustenance — it creates spaces where people discover belonging, reignite creativity, and step into leadership roles through collaborative cooking.

For participants
  • Belonging: recurring, judgment-free friendships
  • Skill acquisition: hands-on cooking and peer knowledge exchange
  • Creative expression: post-meal artistic activities
  • Psychological safety: a low-pressure, welcoming space to learn
  • Economic access: no financial gatekeeping
  • Identity: the transition from learner to expert builder
For volunteers & staff
  • Purpose: meaningful mentorship
  • Leadership: a path from participant to contributor to facilitator
  • Cultural affirmation: sharing heritage recipes in an affirming context
  • Ownership: long-term members guide how the service evolves
  • Network: connections with sponsors and allied organizations
For the broader community
  • Reduced social isolation, and the health costs that come with it
  • Cross-cultural understanding built over shared meals
  • A pipeline of civically engaged community members
  • A replicable, low-barrier model for community infrastructure
Core animating values
  • Growth: every meal offers something new to learn
  • Fulfillment: nourishing others nourishes yourself
  • Discomfort: honoring the courage it takes to show up
  • Passion: enthusiasm that's contagious
  • Connection: every table starts a new relationship

Section 02Research Insights

What we learned about reciprocal learning. We used mixed methods: two semi-structured interviews, three observational studies (an art studio portfolio review, LinkedIn Learning workshops), a competitive platform analysis, a two-hour co-design workshop, and AI-assisted synthesis across four large language models.

Shared routines create return

"They always know to start with scales, so much so that I don't even have to tell them, and they'll sit down and they'll start."Music teacher interview

Predictable rituals build newcomer confidence and give veterans a sense of ownership.

Belonging is the primary need

"Space fulfills the need of … yeah, like, belonging and, like, not being alone, and having a place to create."Pottery volunteer interview, P1

Participants consistently framed community spaces as meeting a fundamental need, not a secondary one.

Skilled volunteering requires scaffolding

Expert volunteers need orientation and trust-building before they can safely contribute (manager of makerspaces interview).

People watch before they ask

Participants default to observation over inquiry in learning environments (HCDE portfolio review field observation).

Safety unlocks participation

Playful, low-stakes framing encouraged experimentation; competitive framing caused people to withdraw (co-design sessions).

Informal teaching works best

Expert teachers naturally used analogies, physical demonstration, and humor — and produced better engagement than formal instruction (co-design sessions).

Synthesis

People seek practical skill development and interest-based connection but rarely find a clear path to contribute. Existing platforms emphasize content consumption and expert instruction, which limits reciprocal exchange and community stewardship. Starting Point addresses that gap with a visible progression: newcomer → participant → contributor → leader.

Section 03Co-Design Outcomes

What a two-hour co-design session revealed. Nine participants, from freshmen to post-grads, brought skills from piano to fly fishing to dance. The goal: reduce perceived stakes, demonstrate universal value, and observe how skills get passed informally.

Party trick share

Each participant presented a niche skill, which normalized informal expertise and put lived experience ahead of credentials.

Balloon & dance: teaching what you just learned

Teams learned a skill — balloon animals, or a dance — then peer-taught it five minutes later, to assess how people pass on a skill before they feel they've mastered it.

Drawing telephone: telestrations

Participants cycled written phrases through rounds of drawing and reinterpretation, to reveal how communication degrades through a chain of interpretation.

Sneaky skill: untrained teaching in action

One participant taught finger-snapping using natural analogies, hands-on help, and humor, with no formal training — to observe how effective unscripted teaching can be.

Spaghetti tower: building with different goals

Two teams built towers under different framings of success — tallest versus coolest — to see how framing shapes participation, creativity, and engagement.

Ten insights from the session

  1. Peer teaching requires very little expertise
  2. Informal strategies — analogies, demonstration, humor — increase engagement
  3. Meaning shifts quickly through a chain of interpretation
  4. Group dynamics outweigh individual skill level
  5. Psychological safety has to come before participation
  6. Reflection deepens insight beyond what observation alone gives
  7. Peer observation needs design support, not just an open invitation to ask
  8. How success is framed shapes effort and creativity
  9. Teaching something unfamiliar builds empathy for the learner
  10. Diverse backgrounds generate unexpected connections

Reflection

Energy stayed high throughout; sequencing the activities prevented repetition; a warm icebreaker created psychological safety early. The richest insights came out during debriefs. Spaghetti Tower sparked the strongest conversation about group dynamics; Sneaky Skill revealed the most organic teaching approaches.

What we'd do differently

Recruit beyond university age ranges next time, especially adults who lose hobby-building after college and feel that loss acutely. And record or transcribe the debriefs, so quotes get captured without pulling the facilitator's attention away.

Section 09Narrative Workshop

Role-playing the service in a real kitchen. A 2.5-hour roleplay with 10 participants, including the design team. We taught the history of empanadas, prepared the ingredients, shaped and baked them, then shared the meal.

Cooking worked as a social lubricant — it broke the ice, sparked conversation, started connections, and opened the door to creative expression and sharing.

Narrative insight

The ambient conversation in the kitchen turned out to be more valuable than polished video documentation, despite how hard the audio and video were to produce.

Service gap

Planning and organization emerged as the real complexity, and the blueprint needed more detail on staffing, materials, timing, and who carries the facilitation burden.

A future artifact

An organizer's journey map would make the resourcing and staffing needs visible.

An audience shift

Feedback from participants pushed the demographic focus toward people facing food insecurity.

Repositioning the service

This distinguishes Starting Point from a food-provision service: it addresses Maslow's hierarchy through dignity, belonging, skill-building, and the chance to contribute.

Sustainability

The value to the community is enough to justify donations and grants that support a dedicated physical space.

How the design question evolved

What began as a broad inquiry into skill-sharing narrowed into a specific challenge: How might we create a financially accessible, human-centered cooking service where people facing food insecurity can gain practical skills, share cultural knowledge, feel dignity instead of need, and eventually transition from guest to contributor?

Section 10Principles & Requirements

Six requirements, drawn from research, co-design, and field observation, each grounded in evidence and tied to the principles that keep the design honest.

Service values

  • Community: validated, interest-based user communities
  • User flexibility: agency and freedom in how people engage
  • Financial accessibility: free, or suggested donation
  • Accessible experience: WCAG compliance, color audits, usability testing
  • Engaging design: validated satisfaction and meaningful engagement
  • Trustworthy service: safe, credible skill development
  • Meaningful experience: participants feel their time was worthwhile
  • Perspective widening: exposure to new skills, cultures, and ways of thinking

1. Build shared routines that bring people back

"They always know to start with scales, so much so that I don't even tell them, and they'll sit down and they'll start."Music teacher interview

Recurring, predictable rituals reduce a newcomer's anxiety and build a returning member's sense of ownership. The service needs consistent structures that participants come to internalize.

2. Foster community & belonging

"Space fulfills the need of … yeah, like, belonging and, like, not being alone, and having a place to create."Pottery volunteer, P1

Community-building is the output, not a byproduct of it. Every design decision should be asked whether it supports belonging.

3. Support skilled volunteers

"They need to know a lot to be a woodshop volunteer … it's kind of a big commitment."Manager of makerspaces, P2

Expert contributors need onboarding, trust-building, and ongoing support. The service has to build a path from participant to skilled volunteer.

4. Balance instruction with open practice

"Students frequently interact with each other by sharing feedback, observing others' reviews, and building off shared critiques."HCDE portfolio review field observation

Learning deepens through peer interaction, not just expert instruction. Event formats need to make room for peer observation, sharing, and building together.

5. Limit barriers to entry

"Even this, like, having free drop-in programs, like, that is mutual aid … and that is, like, a really big core of who we are."Pottery volunteer

Pay-what-you-can pricing, dietary accommodation, multilingual support, and welcoming framing aren't optional extras — they're structural, and they define who Starting Point is.

6. Enable the move from learner to contributor

"There is a clear mix of nervousness and excitement, as students want to make a strong impression while also being eager to learn."HCDE portfolio review field observation

The most powerful moment is the first time a participant teaches someone else. The service should deliberately create that moment, and honor it when it happens.

Design principles

  • The table is the interface. Every touchpoint should feel warm, unhurried, human — like a shared meal. If an interaction would feel cold at a dinner table, it doesn't belong in Starting Point.
  • Make watching easy, asking optional. Since observation comes before participation, design has to make peer-watching effortless. Asking directly is one path in, never the only one.
  • Confidence over perfection. Send people home feeling capable, not necessarily skilled. Celebrate progress over polish.
  • Design for the transition, not just the event. Each event is one step in a longer path. The service should actively design the move from participant to regular to volunteer to leader, not leave it to chance.

AI decision: keep the core service human

Starting Point prioritizes person-to-person interaction, human mentorship, community leadership, and belonging. Adding AI would add training, maintenance, and support burden without strengthening the in-person service at its core. A person should welcome a first-timer, not a chatbot. Any future AI tool could only support the humans doing that work — with transparency, human oversight, and a user's ability to override it — never replace them.
