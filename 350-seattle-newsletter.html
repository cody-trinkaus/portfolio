Section 01TL;DR

350 Seattle's leadership named internal communication as its biggest unsolved problem. Our research traced it to one gap: members have no shared, low-effort way to see what the organization is doing, who is doing it, and how to get involved. We redesigned the newsletter, the org's most trusted channel, into a submission form, an interactive news board and a companion podcast. When the communications director role was eliminated, I took on a contract to rebuild the system so it no longer depends on one person, and I'm implementing it now.

Section 02Context

  • 350 Seattle is a grassroots, non-hierarchical climate justice organization: 8 staff collective members, 9+ teams and campaigns, thousands of supporters. Coordination runs on informal networks and the goodwill of volunteers who are already stretched thin.
  • After COVID, the in-person, word-of-mouth channels gave way to asynchronous tools, and communication never adapted as the org added paid staff and team leads.
  • The newsletter had the lowest unsubscribe rate of any 350 channel and was the only email type that consistently generated donations. So we redesigned within it rather than replacing it.
"How might we use existing streams of communication at 350 Seattle to intervene creatively in support of team-to-team understanding?"Guiding design question

Section 03Research

8 semi-structured interviews17 survey participants3 field observations7 thematic pain points

Interviews ran 45–60 minutes with five staff, three volunteers and one team lead, recruited by snowball sampling. Field observations covered a Policy Action Zoom meeting, a volunteer-pathways discussion, and Climate Community Night, including sitting in on New Member Orientation to feel the onboarding experience firsthand.

Horizontal communication scored lowest, for every role

We used a reduced Communication Satisfaction Questionnaire, adapted from Fields (2002), rating five dimensions — Communication Climate, Supervisory Communication, Horizontal & Informal Communication, Organizational Integration, and Media Quality — on a 1–7 scale. Team leads were most satisfied, then volunteers, then staff. But across every role, horizontal communication — communication across teams — scored the lowest. That confirmed cross-team invisibility as the core issue, not a symptom, and pointed the design squarely at it.

"People don't actually know the other people in the organization or even what other teams exist."Participant 8

A deliberate, critical use of AI

We in-vivo coded our interviews so our findings stayed tied to participants' own words. When we asked Claude to check transcripts for anything we'd missed, its output stayed grounded in what people actually said, which guarded against hallucination.

Section 04Synthesis

Seven thematic pain points

  • Teams operate in silos: little awareness of what other teams do; collaboration only via pre-existing relationships.
  • Limited capacity: past coordination efforts collapsed from lack of bandwidth, not bad ideas.
  • Onboarding failures: joining is exciting but overwhelming, with no consistent pathway across teams.
  • Desire for a central hub: information is fragmented across Signal, Slack, email, Zoom and Docs.
  • In-person as irreplaceable: digital tools supplement relationships; they don't substitute for them.
  • Implicit norms: no documented, org-wide norms for sharing information; each team improvises.
  • Relational trust: people don't communicate across teams largely because they don't know each other.

Co-design: Climate Community Night

We asked participants to draw and write their answer to: "If there was one place to find all active projects at 350, what would it look like?" Four themes emerged: centralized info (a single, low-effort place to see active projects, teams, contacts and ways to get involved), community beyond tasks (rebuilding the informal connection lost since the pandemic), cross-team fluidity (discovering other teams and finding a real foothold in them, not a static directory), and personality & energy (members want 350 to feel "fun," "silly" and "warm" — culture, not just logistics).

Section 05Definition

We practiced participatory design with the communications director, looping him into the process for feedback, and later brought in the executive director and another staff contributor. We weren't able to recruit a volunteer, but every member of our design team was also a newsletter reader, so that stakeholder group was covered from inside the team.

Stakeholder journeys

Communications director

Key pain point: a 4–6 hour process consolidating inconsistent submissions into one voice; staff are late, and prioritization creates an unintended hierarchy.

Opportunity: a single-voice resource; one place for submissions; a better solution for information hierarchy.

Reader

Key pain point: dense content and inconsistent formatting cause information overload; most readers engage with only the first item or two.

Opportunity: keep content concise and scannable; organize sections consistently; clear, even formatting.

Contributor

Key pain point: no submission format or length guidance; negotiating a spot is fraught, with no feedback on whether a contribution mattered.

Opportunity: a simple submission template with writing guidance; report impact back to contributors.

Design requirements

Requirements to fix
  • Provide equitable visibility for team activity; make prioritization transparent
  • Increase publishing cadence beyond once a month
  • Streamline submissions into a unified voice and consistent layout
  • Strengthen cross-org understanding of teams' work
  • Give contributors direct feedback on submissions
  • Encourage readers to explore work across teams
  • Deliver concise updates, clear CTAs, discoverable events
Requirements to keep
  • Solicit content from every part of the organization
  • Keep content curated and approved by the communications director
  • Serve as a consistent home for current happenings
  • Function as a system for sharing on a set cadence

Guiding principles: reduce effort to stay informed · consolidate rather than add channels · integrate with existing channels · stay readable and non-pestering.

Section 06Key Decisions

1. Where to intervene

Alternatives considered: redesigning Climate Community Night, a dedicated outreach team, merch, a lending library, a new event.

What decided it: capacity. Past coordination efforts collapsed from lack of bandwidth, so we chose an avenue 350 already maintained rather than adding a new channel.

2. Newsletter over bulletin board, app or event

Alternatives considered: a digital or physical bulletin board, a team-updates app, a standalone event — each another destination to maintain.

What decided it: trust. The newsletter had the lowest unsubscribe rate of any channel and reliably generated donations, so we redesigned within it rather than replacing it.

3. Three features, not one

A single change, like reformatting alone, would fix readability but leave submission burden and reader fatigue untouched.

What decided it: stakeholders. Each pain point mapped to a different stakeholder — the form, the news board and the podcast together balanced clarity, representation and engagement.

Section 07The Capstone Outcome

The capstone converged on a redesigned newsletter system built on three interconnected features, each tied to a stakeholder's pain point.

1. Interactive news board

A visual collage where every team got an equally prominent tile that expanded into a fuller update — addressing reader overload and the unintended hierarchy of top-of-email placement. It included an embedded calendar and an archive of past issues.

2. Structured submission form

A Google Form that replaced ad hoc email and Slack submissions, so content arrived close to publication-ready and gave contributors a consistent template to work from.

3. Companion podcast

Solo-host and team-interview formats, for members who wanted a synchronous way to connect with the organization.

Section 08The Pivot

What happened

After the capstone, 350 Seattle had to eliminate its communications director position in layoffs. The whole design assumed that person would curate submissions, keep one voice, and send each issue. The process book had already flagged 350's limited technical capacity, and EveryAction's limits, as the biggest risks to the design. Now the person the system depended on was gone.

What I did

I signed an independent contractor agreement with 350 Seattle (Nov 5 – Dec 31, 2026) to implement the system myself. The design question shifted from "how do we make the comms director's job easier?" to "how does this run with no comms director, maintained by volunteers who aren't developers?"

How the design changed

Capstone plan vs. implementation now
Capstone planImplementation now
Comms director chases late submissionsAutomated reminders to teams that haven't submitted, from a roster in the Sheet
Comms director rewrites submissions into one voice (4–6 hrs)Submissions validated on intake — contributors are emailed automatically if something is missing or too long — then AI-drafted into a house voice with the Claude API, for a human to review and approve
Comms director lays out the issueA custom newsletter-builder site compiles approved content into the news board in the brand style
Comms director approves and sendsA volunteer does one final check of the compiled board
Newsletter built in EveryAction's email editorEveryAction can't produce the news board aesthetic, and Gmail strips much of the CSS anyway — so the interactive board lives on 350's WordPress site, and the EveryAction email carries a short summary plus a clickable preview image linking to it
Archive maintained by handPublishing to WordPress is automated through its REST API, so each issue becomes a post and the archive builds itself

The ten-step workflow

  1. 01Reminders go outAutomated
  2. 02Teams submit through the formVolunteer
  3. 03AI drafts blurbs and the email summaryAutomated
  4. 04Volunteer reviews and approvesVolunteer
  5. 05Builder site compiles the news boardAutomated
  6. 06Volunteer final checkVolunteer
  7. 07Publish to WordPressAutomated
  8. 08Volunteer exports the preview imageVolunteer
  9. 09Volunteer builds and sends the email in EveryAction — summary, image, linkVolunteer
  10. 10Row marked SentVolunteer

Automation takes the rote work; the volunteer keeps the judgment calls. Volunteers review, approve, check and send.

Constraints I designed around

  • No developer on staff: every setting a volunteer might need to change lives in one config file, plus a custom menu in the Sheet and a plain-language README.
  • EveryAction stays the system of record for the list and unsubscribes, so there's no list sync and no compliance risk.
  • WordPress can strip scripts from posts: the tiles expand with native <details> elements and need no JavaScript.
  • Accessibility: an image-based email is invisible to screen readers and to clients that block images. So the email has descriptive alt text, a text summary, and a plain "view in browser" link.

Section 10How I'll Know It's Working

These are planned measures, not results — nothing below has been tracked yet.

  • Time to produce an issue (baseline: 4–6 hours of communications-director work)
  • Share of teams submitting each cycle
  • Click-through from the email to the full board
  • Tile-level engagement across teams, compared with the old top-of-email drop-off
  • Unsubscribe rate holding steady
  • Whether volunteers can run an issue without me

Section 11Reflection So Far

TODO: Cody to revise in my voice — the draft below is a starting point, not final copy.

Losing the comms director put a principle from our own research to the test: past efforts at 350 collapsed from a lack of capacity. A design that depends on one person risks repeating that pattern, and I only recognized how real that risk was once I was the one left holding the system.

"Consolidate rather than add" held up. Keeping EveryAction and WordPress instead of introducing new tools is what made the pivot workable at all — there was no new platform to teach anyone, just a new way of filling the ones they already use.

Moving from designer to implementer changed what "done" means to me. A prototype persuades people that an idea could work. A system has to survive handoff — to volunteers, to time, to the org forgetting I was ever involved.

The work isn't finished, and I don't want this page to read like it is. There's no launch yet, no evaluation, and the first real test is whether a volunteer who isn't me can run an issue.
